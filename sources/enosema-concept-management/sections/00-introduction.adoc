== Introduction

Organizations of every kind manage concepts: standards bodies manage the
concepts their documents define; regulators manage the concepts their rules
use; enterprises manage the concepts their systems and data models share;
registries manage reference data and code lists; terminology teams manage
the vocabulary of entire domains.

The failure modes are as universal as the activity. References break when
concepts change. Nobody can say who may change a concept, or on what
basis. History is lost, so consumers cannot trust a citation. Two systems
disagree about what a shared concept means, and no procedure exists for
resolving the disagreement.

Management system standards answer this class of problem by specifying
requirements for the *system* — the policy, governance, processes, and
evaluation — rather than for the product. ISO 9001 does this for quality
management: any organization, of any size and any sector, can adopt it,
conform to it, and be audited against it. The requirements are generic;
the implementation is the organization's own.

This document applies that model to concept management. It specifies
requirements for a *concept management system* (CMS): the set of
governance arrangements, processes, and records by which an organization
manages the concepts it is responsible for — their creation, definition,
registration, localization, relation, revision, and retirement — over
time and across systems.

The requirements are written so that:

* any organization can adopt this document and claim conformity, in whole
or through the requirements classes of Annex <<req-classes>>;

* conformity can be assessed, internally or by a third party, using the
evaluation requirements of <<evaluation>>;

* the concept records produced under a conforming system are fit for
registration in registers operated under the FERIN framework
(ISO 19135 procedures), and can interoperate with terminology management
systems implementing EFS 2501, EFS 2504, EFS 2507, EFS 2510, and this
document.

This document states requirements. It does not prescribe tools, formats,
or organizational structures beyond what governance requires. Where this
document says *shall*, the requirement is mandatory for conformity; where
it says *should*, the requirement is recommended and its absence is a
nonconformity only when the organization cannot demonstrate that its
alternative achieves the same outcome.
