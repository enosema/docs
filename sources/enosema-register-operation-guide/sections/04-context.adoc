[[context]]
== Operating context and register scope

This clause gives guidance for the requirements of EFS 2513, clause 4.

=== Choosing what the register is for

Before any record is created, write down the register's purpose in one
sentence that names the audience and the decision the register
supports. Registers that skip this sentence accumulate concepts nobody
uses and cannot answer the question "`why does this item exist?`" at
review time.

Useful forms of the sentence:

* "`The X register provides the authoritative set of Y used by Z when
they do W.`"

* "`This register is the single point of reference for concepts
defined in the documents of organization Q.`"

From the purpose sentence, derive the register type. The common
patterns:

[cols="1,1,1",options="header"]
|===
|Pattern
|What it holds
|Main consumers

|Vocabulary register
|Controlled vocabulary: relation types, statuses, categories
|Systems that validate or exchange data

|Terminology register
|Domain concepts with designations and definitions
|Authors, translators, content systems

|Reference-data register
|Code lists and item sets with lifecycle
|Interoperating systems, data pipelines

|Standards-derivative register
|Concepts extracted from published documents
|Readers of the documents, tool vendors
|===

A register may be several of these at once; record the mix, because it
changes the review emphasis (terminology registers review definitions
hardest; reference-data registers review lifecycle hardest).

=== Audience analysis worksheet

For each audience, record the answers to five questions. The answers
drive the register's scope, publication formats, and stability
commitments.

. *Who* consumes the concepts — roles, not individuals.

. *What decisions do they make* with the concepts?

. *What breaks for them* if a concept changes: translations, schemas,
published citations, training material?

. *What format do they need* — browsable pages, CSV, TTL, JSON-LD,
TBX, a GCR package?

. *How do they cite* a concept — by identifier, by term, by document
clause?

[example]
The ES Relation Vocabulary register, from the purpose sentence
outward:

* Purpose: "`provides the authoritative set of concept relation types
used in ES concept systems and exchanged between terminology
management systems.`"

* Audiences: terminology management systems (validate relation type
identifiers), standards authors (cite relation types when drafting),
SKOS consumers (project vocabularies).

* Breakage: a renamed relation type identifier breaks validating
systems — hence the stability commitment (clause 6) and the
never-rename rule (clause 7).

* Formats: browsable site, TTL, JSON-LD, CSV (EFS 2516 bulk exports).

* Citation: by URN `urn:enosema:relation-type:<identifier>` and by the
identifier itself in EFS 2510, clause 6.

=== Boundary decisions

Two boundaries are worth deciding explicitly and writing down, because
they are the most common sources of scope disputes later:

* *What is not in the register.* Name the nearest topics that are
excluded. A boundary by exclusion is checkable; a boundary by
impression is not.

* *Where concepts come from.* A register that only standardizes its own
concepts reviews differently from one that adopts concepts from other
bodies. For adopted concepts, record the source and the mapping relation
type (`exact_match`, `close_match`, …) at intake, not later.

=== Context records

Keep the following as controlled records (a directory in the register
repository is enough):

* the purpose sentence and register type;

* the audience worksheet;

* the two boundary decisions; and

* the date of the last review of these records.

EFS 2513 (4.3) requires the scope to be reviewed when the context
changes. Practical trigger list: a new consumer class appears; a
consuming system is retired; the organization's charter or product
scope changes; a review interval elapses (clause 11).

=== Stakeholder interview question bank

When preparing the audience worksheet (<<context>>), interview real
consumers rather than assuming. The questions that produce usable
answers:

. "`Show me the last time you used one of our concepts. What did you
look at, and what were you looking for?`" — surfaces real entry points
(pages, exports, citations) instead of imagined ones.

. "`What would you do if this concept's definition changed tomorrow?`"
— surfaces breakage: translations, schemas, training material.

. "`How do you currently check that you are using the right term?`"
— surfaces validation needs (machine-checkable identifiers) versus
lookup needs (search, browse).

. "`Which concepts do you use most? Least?`" — surfaces the depth
planning inputs of clause 6.

. "`What do we publish that you cannot use?`" — surfaces format gaps
and the publication profile requirements.

. "`Who else in your organization consumes these?`" — surfaces the
second-order audience that scope planning usually misses.

Record the interviews with dates; the worksheet cites them. A scope
statement grounded in six interviews survives review far better than
one grounded in plausibility.

=== Register scope — worked boundary statement

The two boundary decisions of <<context>>, stated for a concrete
register:

* *Excluded by name*: designation-level relations (those are recorded
in the datasets that use this vocabulary, not in the vocabulary
itself); per-deployment deployment metadata; and any relation type not
yet accepted by the control body (proposals live in the issue tracker
until decided).

* *Origin*: all items originate in the register's own decisions; items
adopted from other bodies enter through a proposal that cites the
source and states the mapping relation (`exact_match`,
`close_match`, …) at intake.
